The formula "money and value" undoubtedly invites flights of philosophical and moral fancy. Only very rarely, however, do these lead to relatively coherent conclusions. I will thus concentrate on some historical scenes reflecting the complex relationship between money and value, and between "politics and ethics," two related pairs of terms.
For Sir William Petty (1623-1687), one of the first modern economists, money was the fat of the body politic: too much hinders its agility, while too little makes it prone to illness When philosophers and economists contemplated this issue again a century later, quite a few of them believed money to have an imaginary value only. Anne Robert Turgot (1727-1781), finance minister under Louis XVI and a much discussed author of his time, contradicted this view. In his study on "Valeurs et monnaie" he defended the honour of money. Given his func-tion, he had a great interest in ascribing not only value but also credibility to money, which was the lubricant of his tax system and a fundamental necessity for lavish government spending.
However, history proved the opposite: the deeper money permeated society, the economy and politics, the more severe the crises and phases were in which the value of money shrank like the proverbial "Peau de chagrin" (Balzac 1832).
Money became synonymous with insecurity or even fraud.

Although economic science has generated a vast number of discourses, money as the force or spirit behind historical developments is hard to grasp. / agree with the great French historian Fernand Braudel, who considered money to be simply a private and public instrument of power. Indeed, money represents a value only if it has been officially minted, that is to say marked by public authority and power. For this reason, the right of coinage, i. e. the prerogative to mint coins, was one of the principal sovereign rights a few centuries ago. In view of this, it is hardly surprising that coins were meant to gain credibility primarily through the portrait of the head of state or another symbol of national sovereignty imprinted on them rather than through the denomination number, or value.
This almost magical relationship of power, symbol, monetary value and trust is expressed in a revealing anecdote of Switzerland's history.
